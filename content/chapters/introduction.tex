\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

The deployment of artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly an infrastructure problem as well as a modelling problem. Once a model can perform a task with adequate accuracy, its practical value also depends on whether it can serve the required demand within acceptable latency, cost, and energy budgets. For GPU-accelerated inference, this raises a question that is distinct from achieving the highest performance on a single request: how can a platform use its available accelerators efficiently while accommodating multiple applications with different requirements?

This thesis investigates that question in Kubernetes-based environments, focusing on NVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) and Dynamic Resource Allocation (DRA). The central premise is that resource partitioning, resource allocation, and application behaviour must be considered together: a more flexible infrastructure is useful only if its capabilities match the workload.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Graphics processing units have become a fundamental resource for contemporary AI systems. Their parallel execution capabilities make them well suited to the numerical operations underlying neural networks, but the expansion of accelerated computing also increases the importance of power delivery, cooling, and capacity planning. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimated that data centers consumed approximately 415~TWh of electricity in 2024, corresponding to about 1.5\% of global electricity consumption. Its 2025 base-case projection put data center demand at approximately 945~TWh in 2030, with accelerated servers accounting for almost half of the projected net increase. These figures cover data centres as a whole, not GPUs alone, and the 2030 value is a scenario-based projection rather than an observed outcome~\cite{iea2025energyai}.

The scale of the hardware involved helps explain why inefficient use matters. NVIDIA describes a GB200 NVL72 rack with 72 GPUs and an approximate full-load power requirement of 120~kW, including the compute nodes and other rack components~\cite{nvidia2026rackpower}. This is a rack-level power figure, not the consumption of an individual accelerator or a measure of wasted energy. Nevertheless, it illustrates how allocating additional GPU capacity can entail substantial infrastructure requirements beyond purchasing the devices themselves. For an infrastructure operator, the relevant cost includes the supporting system and its operation, not simply the price of a GPU.

Cloud deployment changes the accounting model without eliminating the consequences of idle capacity. Google Cloud, for example, bills Compute Engine GPU resources according to provisioned time and continues to charge a running instance even when it is idle~\cite{google2026computepricing}. Thus, reducing the number of allocated devices or shortening the time needed to complete a fixed amount of work can have economic value.

\subsection{Not Every AI Workload is Equal}
\label{subsec:workloads-equality}
A crucial complication is that AI workloads are not homogeneous. At one end of the spectrum, large-scale training can require many GPUs to cooperate on a single model. In this case model parameters, activations, and optimizer state may exceed the memory available on one device, while the computational volume motivates distributing work across a cluster. Megatron-LM demonstrates the combination of tensor, pipeline, and data parallelism to train very large language models across thousands of GPUs, while also exposing the communication and synchronization costs of such scaling~\cite{narayanan2021megatron}. Allocating multiple dedicated accelerators is therefore justified for some workloads, even if, high allocation does not necessarily imply that every device remains computationally saturated.

At the other end of the spectrum are smaller inference services: compact language models, image or text classifiers, embedding generation, and anomaly-detection pipelines. TinyLlama, for example, is a language model with 1.1 billion parameters~\cite{zhang2024tinyllama} and can be defined as a Small Language Model. Such applications motivate investigating allocations smaller than a complete high-end GPU. They do not, however, justify assuming that every small model has a small operational footprint. Required memory and execution capacity also depend on numerical precision, input size, concurrent requests, batching, and the latency target. A model that fits into a small memory partition may still be constrained by that partition's compute capacity or memory bandwidth.

Inference also introduces temporal heterogeneity. An interactive service may remain deployed while handling intermittent requests, whereas an offline embedding task may process a finite dataset and then terminate. Request lengths can differ substantially within the same language-model service. Provisioning for peak demand can leave capacity unused during quieter periods, while provisioning too aggressively can increase queueing delays during bursts. The infrastructure must therefore accommodate differences both between applications and over time, rather than treating every GPU consumer as a permanently saturated training job.

\subsection{Allocation vs. Actual Utilization}
\label{subsec:allocation-vs-utilization}
Evidence from production clusters illustrates the gap between reserving devices and using them effectively. In their study of Microsoft's Philly cluster, Jeon et al.\ report a mean GPU utilization of 52.32\% across the analysed training jobs, despite the exclusive allocation of devices to those jobs. The authors identify synchronization and interference in shared system resources among the causes of reduced utilization~\cite[Section~3.2 and Table~3]{jeon2019philly}. This is a historical observation from a particular training environment, not a universal estimate for present-day inference platforms. A later study of Alibaba's machine-learning platform, covering more than 6,000 GPUs, likewise identifies low GPU utilization, scheduling constraints, and queueing delays as operational challenges~\cite{weng2022mlaas}. Together, these studies motivate measuring the difference between allocated capacity and useful work rather than treating device occupancy in a scheduler as sufficient evidence of efficiency.

These considerations make GPU management in the cloud a multi-objective design problem. \emph{Resource efficiency} requires matching allocations to actual demand and avoiding capacity that is reserved but unusable by other applications. \emph{Scalability} requires supporting increasing concurrency and changing traffic without assuming that every additional service replica needs another complete accelerator. \emph{Flexibility} concerns using the available hardware for different workload profiles, while \emph{agility} concerns how quickly allocations and deployments can respond when those profiles or their priorities change.

Efficiency must also coexist with \emph{reliability}: an application should not unexpectedly lose its service quality because a neighbouring workload changes behaviour, and failures must be handled with clearly understood recovery procedures. \emph{Simplified management} calls for consistent provisioning, configuration, and observability rather than isolated manual device assignments. Finally, \emph{future-proofing} means reducing unnecessary coupling between application specifications and a particular hardware configuration. It does not mean that new accelerators can be adopted without driver, compatibility, or application validation. These goals can conflict: finer sharing may improve density while complicating isolation, and maintaining recovery headroom may reduce average utilization. A useful platform must make those trade-offs explicit.

\subsection{Kubernetes as Running Platform: GPUs are not CPUs}
\label{subsec:kubernetes-running-platform}
Kubernetes provides a relevant setting for this problem because it offers a common orchestration model for containerized services and batch workloads. However, its familiar CPU and memory resource abstractions do not transfer unchanged to accelerators. CPU requests can be expressed in fractional units, while requests and limits allow workloads to share a node under controlled policies. Memory has different enforcement behaviour, including the possibility of out-of-memory termination; it should not be described as freely interchangeable with CPU time. Scheduling is based on declared resource requirements rather than a guarantee of continuous, automatic migration according to observed load~\cite{kubernetes135resources}.

Kubernetes does not require every GPU allocation in every configuration to correspond to an entire physical card. A device plugin can expose MIG devices, and NVIDIA also provides time-slicing configurations that advertise shared access~\cite{nvidia2026timeslicing}. The issue is the granularity and semantics of the advertised resource: the default whole-device configuration provides coarse allocation, while finer sharing depends on additional mechanisms and explicit policy. It is therefore more accurate to describe a mismatch between workload needs and the conventional allocation abstraction than to claim that GPUs are intrinsically indivisible or that Kubernetes cannot support sharing.

The natural response is to consolidate compatible workloads, but consolidation must be controlled. Sharing an accelerator without considering memory capacity, interference, admission policy, and fault behaviour can turn unused capacity into unpredictable service. For interactive inference, an increase in aggregate throughput is not sufficient if request latency becomes unacceptable. For multi-user platforms, administrators also need to understand what isolation is provided by the GPU mechanism and what remains the responsibility of the surrounding system.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The objective of this thesis is to design, implement, and evaluate a Kubernetes-based approach to improving GPU utilization for AI inference using NVIDIA MIG and DRA. Improvement is understood in terms of useful application work and resource efficiency, subject to acceptable performance and isolation requirements. The work does not assume that partitioning is preferable to using a complete GPU, or that replacing device-plugin allocation with DRA necessarily increases throughput.

The first objective is to identify when consolidation of lightweight inference workloads is beneficial. The corresponding proof of concept deploys multiple Ollama instances serving TinyLlama and compares a single instance on the complete GPU, multiple instances sharing the complete GPU, an individual instance restricted to a MIG partition, and multiple instances on separate MIG partitions. This progression distinguishes the cost of restricting an application from the possible benefit of executing several applications concurrently. It also separates per-request behaviour from aggregate infrastructure throughput. Model configuration, request characteristics, and concurrency must be controlled so that the comparison does not attribute a change in the workload to the allocation mechanism.

A second objective is to establish the role of DRA independently of the benefits of MIG. In a long-running Ollama deployment, allocating a MIG device at Pod creation is principally a provisioning operation. Once the service is running, that allocation does not by itself adapt to changing requests. Any performance advantage observed in such a configuration must therefore be analysed in terms of partitioning, consolidation, and serving behaviour, rather than attributed automatically to DRA. The practical question is whether more expressive allocation becomes useful when workloads make repeated, heterogeneous resource requests.

To investigate that question, the second proof of concept considers batch inference managed through Kueue. Kueue controls workload admission and quota consumption, while Pod placement and other lifecycle responsibilities remain with Kubernetes components~\cite{kueue2026overview}. Jobs with different profiles and service classes provide recurring opportunities to select GPU or MIG resources through DRA. The investigation compares explicit static-allocation baselines with a policy that can select suitable resources for successive jobs. Priority is treated as an admission-policy input, not as a guarantee of execution time.

The scope of dynamic behaviour is deliberately bounded. This thesis does not assume that an already running container can transparently exchange or resize its assigned GPU. Adaptation is considered between jobs or executions, with resource reclamation governed by the actual Pod and claim lifecycle. In particular, completion of application work must not be confused with deletion of every associated resource object. Likewise, a benefit obtained by selecting among existing MIG instances must be distinguished from automatic reconfiguration of the physical partition layout.

The experimental platform uses an NVIDIA A100 attached to a Google Cloud virtual machine, a single-node K3s cluster, the NVIDIA GPU Operator, and a Prometheus-based monitoring stack. This testbed supports controlled investigation of the proposed mechanisms, but it does not establish behaviour at multi-node production scale or demonstrate high availability under physical-node failure. The intended contribution is therefore a reproducible implementation and an evidence-based account of the conditions under which MIG and DRA are useful, including conditions under which they offer little benefit or impose unacceptable trade-offs.
