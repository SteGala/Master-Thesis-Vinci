\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

The deployment of artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly an infrastructure problem as well as a modelling problem. Once a model can perform a task with adequate accuracy, its practical value also depends on whether it can serve the required demand within acceptable latency, cost, and energy budgets. For GPU-accelerated inference, this raises a question that is distinct from achieving the highest performance on a single request: how can a platform use its available accelerators efficiently while accommodating multiple applications with different requirements?

This thesis investigates that question in Kubernetes-based environments, focusing on NVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) and Dynamic Resource Allocation (DRA). The central premise is that resource partitioning, resource allocation, and application behaviour must be considered together. A more flexible infrastructure is useful only if its capabilities match the workload and produce benefits that can be demonstrated against appropriate baselines.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Graphics processing units have become a fundamental resource for contemporary AI systems. Their parallel execution capabilities make them well suited to the numerical operations underlying neural networks, but the expansion of accelerated computing also increases the importance of power delivery, cooling, and capacity planning. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimated that data centres consumed approximately 415~TWh of electricity in 2024, corresponding to about 1.5\% of global electricity consumption. Its 2025 base-case projection put data-centre demand at approximately 945~TWh in 2030, with accelerated servers accounting for almost half of the projected net increase. These figures cover data centres as a whole, not GPUs alone, and the 2030 value is a scenario-based projection rather than an observed outcome~\cite{iea2025energyai}.

The scale of the hardware involved helps explain why inefficient use matters. NVIDIA's administration guide describes a GB200 NVL72 rack with 72 GPUs and an approximate full-load power requirement of 120~kW, including the compute nodes and other rack components~\cite{nvidia2026rackpower}. This is a rack-level power figure, not the consumption of an individual accelerator or a measure of wasted energy. Nevertheless, it illustrates how allocating additional GPU capacity can entail substantial infrastructure requirements beyond purchasing the devices themselves. For an infrastructure operator, the relevant cost includes the supporting system and its operation, not simply the price of a GPU.

Cloud deployment changes the accounting model without eliminating the consequences of idle capacity. Google Cloud, for example, bills Compute Engine GPU resources according to provisioned time and continues to charge a running instance even when it is idle~\cite{google2026computepricing}. Thus, reducing the number of allocated devices or shortening the time needed to complete a fixed amount of work can have economic value. Merely increasing a utilization counter while keeping the same resources running for the same duration does not automatically reduce the bill. Similarly, improvements observed at GPU level should not be equated with measured reductions in total data-centre electricity use.

A crucial complication is that AI workloads are not homogeneous. At one end of the spectrum, large-scale training can require many GPUs to cooperate on a single model. Model parameters, activations, and optimizer state may exceed the memory available on one device, while the computational volume motivates distributing work across a cluster. Megatron-LM demonstrates the combination of tensor, pipeline, and data parallelism to train very large language models across thousands of GPUs, while also exposing the communication and synchronization costs of such scaling~\cite{narayanan2021megatron}. Allocating multiple dedicated accelerators is therefore justified for some workloads; even then, high allocation does not necessarily imply that every device remains computationally saturated.

At the other end of the spectrum are smaller inference services: compact language models, image or text classifiers, embedding generation, and anomaly-detection pipelines. TinyLlama, for example, is a language model with 1.1 billion parameters~\cite{zhang2024tinyllama}; sentence-embedding models such as Sentence-BERT support semantic similarity and retrieval through fixed-dimensional representations of text~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}. Such applications motivate investigating allocations smaller than a complete high-end GPU. They do not, however, justify assuming that every small model has a small operational footprint. Required memory and execution capacity also depend on numerical precision, input size, concurrent requests, batching, and the latency target. A model that fits into a small memory partition may still be constrained by that partition's compute capacity or memory bandwidth.

Inference also introduces temporal heterogeneity. An interactive service may remain deployed while handling intermittent requests, whereas an offline embedding task may process a finite dataset and then terminate. Request lengths can differ substantially within the same language-model service. Provisioning for peak demand can leave capacity unused during quieter periods, while provisioning too aggressively can increase queueing delays during bursts. The infrastructure must therefore accommodate differences both between applications and over time, rather than treating every GPU consumer as a permanently saturated training job.

Evidence from production clusters illustrates the gap between reserving devices and using them effectively. In their study of Microsoft's Philly cluster, Jeon et al.\ report a mean GPU utilization of 52.32\% across the analysed training jobs, despite the exclusive allocation of devices to those jobs. The authors identify synchronization and interference in shared system resources among the causes of reduced utilization~\cite[Section~3.2 and Table~3]{jeon2019philly}. This is a historical observation from a particular training environment, not a universal estimate for present-day inference platforms. A later study of Alibaba's machine-learning platform, covering more than 6,000 GPUs, likewise identifies low GPU utilization, scheduling constraints, and queueing delays as operational challenges~\cite{weng2022mlaas}. Together, these studies motivate measuring the difference between allocated capacity and useful work rather than treating device occupancy in a scheduler as sufficient evidence of efficiency.

The term \emph{GPU utilization} itself requires care. NVIDIA defines the GPU utilization reported by \texttt{nvidia-smi} as the fraction of a sampling interval during which at least one kernel executes on the device~\cite{nvidia2026smi}. It is not the fraction of theoretical floating-point throughput achieved, nor a direct measurement of the fraction of all streaming multiprocessors kept busy. A high value can therefore coexist with limited use of compute capacity, for example when execution is constrained by another resource. This thesis consequently treats utilization as one diagnostic signal and relates it to application throughput, latency, memory use, and power measurements. The objective is to increase useful service delivered by the hardware, not to maximize a telemetry value in isolation.

The energy argument also needs to distinguish instantaneous power from energy consumed over an interval. There is no universal proportional relationship between offered load, a GPU utilization percentage, and power draw. Measurements in the ML.ENERGY benchmark show that power depends on the computational characteristics of the model and its execution phases: memory-intensive language-model decoding can draw substantially less power than compute-intensive diffusion inference on the same GPU family. The study also shows that batching can amortize energy across requests, but that overloading a server can increase energy expenditure through additional preemption and recovery work~\cite{chung2025mlenergy}. Thus, the statement that energy consumption does not grow linearly with load is best understood as a warning against assuming proportionality, not as a universal law that every increase in utilization improves efficiency.

For an observation interval of duration \(T\), let \(P(t)\) denote the measured power of the system boundary under consideration, and let \(N\) be the amount of useful work completed during that interval. The corresponding energy and average energy per unit of work are
\begin{equation}
    E = \int_0^T P(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,
    \qquad
    e = \frac{E}{N} = \frac{\overline{P}}{X},
    \label{eq:energy-per-work}
\end{equation}
where \(\overline{P}=E/T\) is average power and \(X=N/T\) is throughput. Depending on the experiment, useful work can be expressed as completed requests or generated tokens, provided that the task, output characteristics, and measurement interval remain comparable.

Equation~\ref{eq:energy-per-work} explains the opportunity offered by consolidation. If useful throughput grows proportionally more than average power, energy per unit of work decreases, even when instantaneous power increases. Conversely, a higher utilization value does not establish this condition. Consolidation can be counterproductive if contention increases execution time, causes repeated work, or violates service-level objectives. Reducing total energy for a fixed workload and reducing energy per request under increased demand are also different outcomes. Measurements must state whether they cover the physical GPU, the host, or a wider infrastructure boundary; GPU-only measurements cannot substantiate a claim about total facility consumption.

These considerations make GPU management in the cloud a multi-objective design problem. \emph{Resource efficiency} requires matching allocations to actual demand and avoiding capacity that is reserved but unusable by other applications. \emph{Scalability} requires supporting increasing concurrency and changing traffic without assuming that every additional service replica needs another complete accelerator. \emph{Flexibility} concerns using the available hardware for different workload profiles, while \emph{agility} concerns how quickly allocations and deployments can respond when those profiles or their priorities change.

Efficiency must also coexist with \emph{reliability}: an application should not unexpectedly lose its service quality because a neighbouring workload changes behaviour, and failures must be handled with clearly understood recovery procedures. \emph{Simplified management} calls for consistent provisioning, configuration, and observability rather than isolated manual device assignments. Finally, \emph{future-proofing} means reducing unnecessary coupling between application specifications and a particular hardware configuration. It does not mean that new accelerators can be adopted without driver, compatibility, or application validation. These goals can conflict: finer sharing may improve density while complicating isolation, and maintaining recovery headroom may reduce average utilization. A useful platform must make those trade-offs explicit.

Kubernetes provides a relevant setting for this problem because it offers a common orchestration model for containerized services and batch workloads. However, its familiar CPU and memory resource abstractions do not transfer unchanged to accelerators. CPU requests can be expressed in fractional units, and requests and limits allow workloads to share a node under controlled policies. Memory has different enforcement behaviour, including the possibility of out-of-memory termination; it should not be described as freely interchangeable with CPU time. Scheduling is based on declared resource requirements rather than a guarantee of continuous, automatic migration according to observed load~\cite{kubernetes135resources}.

In the conventional device-plugin model, GPUs are exposed as extended resources. These resources are requested in integer quantities and cannot be overcommitted through the generic extended-resource mechanism~\cite{kubernetes135deviceplugins}. GPU requests and limits, when both are specified, must be equal~\cite{kubernetesgpuscheduling}. With an NVIDIA device plugin configured to expose whole GPUs without sharing, a request such as \texttt{nvidia.com/gpu: 1} therefore reserves an entire advertised device. If its application uses only a small part of the GPU's capacity, that unused capacity does not become a fractional schedulable resource for another Pod. A second application can remain pending even though the first leaves substantial hardware capacity idle.

This limitation must be stated precisely. Kubernetes does not require every GPU allocation in every configuration to correspond to an entire physical card. A device plugin can expose MIG devices, and NVIDIA also provides time-slicing configurations that advertise shared access~\cite{nvidia2026timeslicing}. The issue is the granularity and semantics of the advertised resource: the default whole-device configuration provides coarse allocation, while finer sharing depends on additional mechanisms and explicit policy. It is therefore more accurate to describe a mismatch between workload needs and the conventional allocation abstraction than to claim that GPUs are intrinsically indivisible or that Kubernetes cannot support sharing.

The natural response is to consolidate compatible workloads, but consolidation must be controlled. Sharing an accelerator without considering memory capacity, interference, admission policy, and fault behaviour can turn unused capacity into unpredictable service. For interactive inference, an increase in aggregate throughput is not sufficient if request latency becomes unacceptable. For multi-user platforms, administrators also need to understand what isolation is provided by the GPU mechanism and what remains the responsibility of the surrounding system.

Different sharing mechanisms address different parts of this problem. Time slicing multiplexes access to a GPU, but NVIDIA's implementation does not provide the memory and fault isolation offered by MIG~\cite{nvidia2026timeslicing}. Multi-Process Service (MPS) enables operations from cooperating CUDA processes to overlap and reduces some context-management overheads~\cite{nvidia2026mps}. MIG instead partitions supported devices into instances with dedicated compute and memory resources, including isolated paths through the memory subsystem~\cite{nvidia2026mig}. These mechanisms represent trade-offs rather than a universal ranking. MIG provides the hardware-partitioning foundation examined in this thesis, but a smaller instance also has fewer resources, and partition boundaries do not remove shared host dependencies or protect against every failure of the underlying physical device.

Hardware partitioning alone does not determine which workload receives which instance. DRA addresses this allocation layer through resource claims, device classes, and attribute-based device selection. In the Kubernetes 1.35 model relevant to this work, claims describe device requirements, while claim templates support generating separate claims for Pods~\cite{kubernetes135dra}. DRA is not itself a GPU execution scheduler, and its presence does not automatically accelerate inference or reconfigure MIG partitions. Its potential value lies in expressing requirements and selecting suitable resources as workloads are submitted and admitted. This distinction motivates evaluating not only long-running inference services, but also workloads whose allocation decisions recur between executions.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The objective of this thesis is to design, implement, and evaluate a Kubernetes-based approach to improving GPU utilization for AI inference using NVIDIA MIG and DRA. Improvement is understood in terms of useful application work and resource efficiency, subject to acceptable performance and isolation requirements. The work does not assume that partitioning is preferable to using a complete GPU, or that replacing device-plugin allocation with DRA necessarily increases throughput.

The first objective is to identify when consolidation of lightweight inference workloads is beneficial. The corresponding proof of concept deploys multiple Ollama instances serving TinyLlama and compares a single instance on the complete GPU, multiple instances sharing the complete GPU, an individual instance restricted to a MIG partition, and multiple instances on separate MIG partitions. This progression distinguishes the cost of restricting an application from the possible benefit of executing several applications concurrently. It also separates per-request behaviour from aggregate infrastructure throughput. Model configuration, request characteristics, and concurrency must be controlled so that the comparison does not attribute a change in the workload to the allocation mechanism.

A second objective is to establish the role of DRA independently of the benefits of MIG. In a long-running Ollama deployment, allocating a MIG device at Pod creation is principally a provisioning operation. Once the service is running, that allocation does not by itself adapt to changing requests. Any performance advantage observed in such a configuration must therefore be analysed in terms of partitioning, consolidation, and serving behaviour, rather than attributed automatically to DRA. The practical question is whether more expressive allocation becomes useful when workloads make repeated, heterogeneous resource requests.

To investigate that question, the second proof of concept considers batch inference managed through Kueue. Kueue controls workload admission and quota consumption, while Pod placement and other lifecycle responsibilities remain with Kubernetes components~\cite{kueue2026overview}. Jobs with different profiles and service classes provide recurring opportunities to select GPU or MIG resources through DRA. The investigation compares explicit static-allocation baselines with a policy that can select suitable resources for successive jobs. Priority is treated as an admission-policy input, not as a guarantee of execution time.

The scope of dynamic behaviour is deliberately bounded. This thesis does not assume that an already running container can transparently exchange or resize its assigned GPU. Adaptation is considered between jobs or executions, with resource reclamation governed by the actual Pod and claim lifecycle. In particular, completion of application work must not be confused with deletion of every associated resource object. Likewise, a benefit obtained by selecting among existing MIG instances must be distinguished from automatic reconfiguration of the physical partition layout.

A third objective is to provide an evaluation that captures both benefits and costs. Relevant measurements include aggregate throughput, request latency, job completion and waiting times, GPU activity and memory use, and energy per unit of useful work where power telemetry supports that calculation. Repeated runs and documented configurations are needed to assess whether differences are reproducible. Operational complexity, compatibility constraints, and the effort required to configure and manage the platform are part of the assessment rather than incidental implementation details.

The experimental platform uses an NVIDIA A100 attached to a Google Cloud virtual machine, a single-node K3s cluster, the NVIDIA GPU Operator, and a Prometheus-based monitoring stack. This testbed supports controlled investigation of the proposed mechanisms, but it does not establish behaviour at multi-node production scale or demonstrate high availability under physical-node failure. The intended contribution is therefore a reproducible implementation and an evidence-based account of the conditions under which MIG and DRA are useful, including conditions under which they offer little benefit or impose unacceptable trade-offs.

\section{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:thesis-structure}

Chapter~\ref{chap:state-of-the-art} reviews related research on improving GPU utilization and positions this work in relation to existing resource-management approaches.

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} introduces the technologies required to understand the proposed platform: GPU sharing, NVIDIA MIG, GPU support in Kubernetes, DRA, the Linux GPU software stack, and the NVIDIA GPU Operator.

Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} presents the architecture through its hardware, allocation, and application layers, explaining the responsibilities assigned to each and their interactions.

Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} describes the testbed, the multiple-Ollama proof of concept, and the Kueue-managed batch scenario. It details their implementation and evaluates the experimental results against the selected baselines.

Finally, Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions} summarizes the findings, discusses their implications and limitations, and outlines future work on automated MIG management and workload-aware resource assignment.
