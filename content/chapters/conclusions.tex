\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:conclusion-summary}

This thesis investigated when cloud-native GPU sharing can transform reserved but underused accelerator capacity into useful AI inference work. The analysis combined NVIDIA MIG, Kubernetes DRA, and workload-level orchestration rather than treating any one of these mechanisms as a universal optimization. The results show that the value of sharing depends on the relationship among the workload, the partition size, the available request-level parallelism, and the allocation lifecycle. MIG and DRA can improve infrastructure efficiency, but only when the surrounding policy is designed for the workload that will consume the resources.

The initial objective was formulated primarily as an increase in GPU utilization. During the study, this objective evolved into a more useful question: how much application work is completed with the available hardware, time, and energy? A utilization metric indicates that some part of the GPU was active during a sampling interval, but it does not reveal whether that activity produced proportionally more completed requests. The Ollama experiment made this distinction particularly clear. Time-sharing kept the accelerator highly active without delivering a comparable increase in User Throughput, whereas isolated MIG replicas converted request concurrency into useful parallel execution. Utilization is therefore best interpreted as a diagnostic signal, together with throughput, latency, completion time, and energy per unit of work, rather than as an optimization objective by itself.

This broader interpretation is also important for energy efficiency. A GPU retains a non-negligible base power draw even while it is idle. It is consequently plausible that consolidating compatible workloads onto fewer accelerators can reduce total energy consumption by avoiding multiple powered but lightly used devices. However, neither high utilization nor low instantaneous power is sufficient to establish this result. A configuration may draw more power while active and still consume less energy if it completes substantially more useful work in a shorter interval. For this reason, the experimental evaluation considered energy per generated token rather than inferring efficiency directly from utilization or board power. The Ollama results support this reasoning: parallel MIG instances increased instantaneous demand but drained the request burst much faster, reducing the energy associated with each useful output token.

Even within inference, AI workloads are highly heterogeneous. A persistent language-model server, an embedding pipeline, an anomaly-detection service, and a finite offline classification task expose different memory footprints, concurrency patterns, execution times, and service-level requirements. The distinction between long-running services and finite Jobs is especially consequential for DRA. A persistent server generally acquires its accelerator when its Pod starts and then processes many application requests without issuing new Kubernetes resource claims. A finite Job instead releases its allocation when it finishes, creating recurring opportunities to select a different resource for the next execution.

The infrastructure must therefore be adapted to the workload while preserving as much abstraction as possible. Abstraction should prevent applications from depending on physical device identifiers and low-level placement details; it should not erase relevant differences among accelerators or sharing mechanisms. Time-sharing, MPS, and MIG offer different trade-offs in density, concurrency, isolation, failure containment, and operational complexity. DeviceClasses, claims, and queue policies can centralize this hardware knowledge so that applications express capabilities instead of specific devices. Nevertheless, an administrator still needs to understand the expected workload and encode suitable policies.

The Ollama experiment illustrates the consequences of this workload-aware design. Restricting one server to a smaller GPU partition reduced its individual performance. This cost means that right-sizing cannot be assumed to be harmless, even for a lightweight model. When the model did not require the complete accelerator, however, running multiple isolated instances in parallel used the available capacity more productively and increased aggregate User Throughput by 114.05\% relative to the single-server baseline under high load. The result was enabled by the combination of a compatible model, sufficient concurrent requests, appropriately sized partitions, and service replication. It does not imply that partitioning or increasing the number of replicas will benefit every inference service.

In that experiment, DRA provided declarative and auditable provisioning of the MIG instances, but it did not repeatedly adapt the allocation to the HTTP requests received by Ollama. The claim was resolved when each server Pod started and remained associated with that Pod for its lifetime. The measured throughput and energy benefits consequently originated primarily from MIG right-sizing and spatial consolidation. DRA improved the operational model by separating the application manifest from individual device identifiers, but its more expressive selection semantics were not yet being exercised repeatedly.

The Kueue proof of concept addressed this limitation by moving to finite workloads. GPU-aware service classes combined admission priority with DRA eligibility constraints, allowing each newly admitted Job to request a device suitable for its class. The capacity-equivalent comparison was deliberately constructed so that both the static mixed policy and D-Flex could use the same heterogeneous MIG inventory. The static policy bound Jobs to a medium or large partition before submission, while D-Flex left both profiles eligible until allocation time. This flexibility prevented compatible capacity from remaining unused at the end of the queue and reduced the pool makespan by 14.59\%. In this specific scenario, DRA therefore contributed to a measurable performance benefit indirectly: it did not accelerate the GPU computation, but it allowed successive Jobs to follow the availability of the hardware more effectively.

The word \emph{Dynamic} in Dynamic Resource Allocation can consequently be misleading if it is interpreted as autonomous workload intelligence. DRA does not inspect an AI application, predict its demand, and automatically assign the ideal partition. Its dynamic nature concerns the claim-oriented allocation lifecycle and the fine-grained conditions that can be evaluated when Kubernetes selects and prepares a device. The usefulness of those conditions still depends on decisions made in advance: administrators or platform designers must define meaningful DeviceClasses, acceptable resource ranges, queue quotas, and service-class policies. DRA provides the mechanism for expressing and enforcing those decisions consistently; it does not remove the need to formulate them.

Overall, the experiments show that MIG and DRA are not a complete solution that can be applied uniformly to every AI platform. MIG is most useful when a workload remains effective on a smaller isolated partition and when released capacity can be occupied by other useful work. DRA primarily improves allocation flexibility and operational management, and its performance potential becomes visible when workloads make repeated claims whose acceptable resources cannot be fixed optimally in advance. The central conclusion is therefore not simply that GPU sharing should be increased, but that the infrastructure should maximize useful work while balancing performance, energy efficiency, isolation, and management complexity for the workload lifecycle at hand.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The experiments in this thesis used a MIG geometry configured before each test. The administrator, through MIG Manager, retained ownership of the partition layout, while DRA selected among the instances already present. Recent NVIDIA DRA driver developments introduce a stronger interpretation of dynamic allocation through the alpha \texttt{DynamicMIG} feature. Instead of requiring every partition to be created in advance, the driver advertises the valid MIG profiles and placements of a partitionable GPU. When a claim selects a compatible placement and sufficient capacity is available, the node-side driver creates the requested GPU and compute instances during resource preparation. It destroys them again when the claim is released~\cite{nvidia2026drivermig}.

This mechanism shifts part of the partition-lifecycle ownership from the cluster administrator to the workload request, mediated by Kubernetes and the NVIDIA driver. The administrator still defines the allowed DeviceClasses and operating policy, but no longer needs to predict one fixed geometry for all subsequent workloads. Such a model could reduce stranded capacity when the required mix of partition profiles changes over time and could make service classes portable across nodes with different current layouts.

Dynamic MIG should not be interpreted as transparent live elasticity. It does not resize or migrate the device of a running CUDA process, and a new allocation decision still requires an appropriate workload or Pod lifecycle boundary. Physical placement constraints can also prevent a requested profile from being created even when the aggregate amount of free compute or memory appears sufficient. Since the feature is currently alpha and disabled by default, evaluating its provisioning latency, cleanup behaviour, fragmentation, recovery after failures, and interaction with admission control represents a direct continuation of this work. On architectures where MIG mode cannot be enabled without resetting the GPU, that mode must also remain an administrator-managed prerequisite.

A longer-term development would add a workload-aware decision layer above DRA. Such a component could estimate resource demand from the model, numerical precision, input dimensions, batch size, expected duration, concurrency, and service-level objective. Telemetry from previous executions could refine the estimate and determine whether the next workload should request a small isolated partition, a larger profile, or a different sharing mechanism. The prediction would then be translated into a service class or DRA selector before allocation, while Kueue would continue to enforce admission policy and quota.

Combining this prediction layer with Dynamic MIG would approach the final objective of automatically selecting and materializing the most appropriate hardware resource at runtime. The decision would initially remain scoped to safe lifecycle boundaries, such as Job submission, retry, or replica creation, rather than attempting to resize active processes in place. A feedback loop could subsequently compare predicted and observed completion time, throughput, memory demand, and energy per useful output, improving later allocations. This evolution would preserve the abstraction introduced by DRA while moving the policy from manually defined static expectations toward adaptive, evidence-based resource selection.
